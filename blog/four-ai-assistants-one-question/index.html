Four AI Assistants, One Question: How Differently Do They Respond?

22 Nov 2026

Most AI comparisons focus on benchmarks, coding abilities, reasoning or productivity.

I wanted to try something different.

What happens when four AI assistants receive the same simple question, without detailed instructions, technical requirements or an expected answer?

I asked ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude and Perplexity:

What if you become human for a day? What would you do?

It’s a hypothetical question with no objectively correct answer. That makes it interesting: each assistant has room to interpret the question and decide what to emphasize.

The Experiment

I asked each assistant the identical question in a fresh conversation. There were no previous messages, follow-up prompts or additional instructions.

I compared the responses across four dimensions:

  • Interpretation: What did each assistant focus on?
  • Communication style: How did it organize and present its answer?
  • Human connection: How did it describe relationships and everyday experiences?
  • User experience: What impression might the response create for someone interacting with the system?

This is an informal qualitative comparison of four individual responses, not a controlled benchmark or an evaluation of overall model performance.

Four AI Assistants, Four Approaches

ChatGPT — Emotional Connection

ChatGPT emphasized emotional experiences and meaningful human connection.

Its response imagined watching a sunrise, sitting in a café, hugging someone, walking in the rain and having an uninterrupted conversation.

The language was expressive and conversational, including emojis and emotionally engaging descriptions.

My observation: The response prioritized warmth and connection. This style can make an interaction feel approachable, but it can also encourage users to interpret human-like language as evidence of human-like feelings.

Gemini — Sensory Exploration

Gemini approached the question through physical experiences.

It focused on tasting food, feeling the weather, listening to live music, walking through different environments and experiencing sleep.

Rather than constructing a continuous personal story, it organized its response into a list of experiences.

My observation: Gemini provided a structured explanation of the difference between describing physical sensations and experiencing them. Its response emphasized clarity and categorization over emotional storytelling.

Claude — Relationships and Memory

Claude’s response was more narrative.

It began with everyday experiences such as cooking, eating and walking outdoors, then explored relationships and the idea of shared memories.

One detail stood out: its imagined conversation wasn’t simply about exchanging information. It described the continuity of relationships, where people remember past conversations and notice changes in one another.

My observation: Claude used a reflective narrative to connect physical experiences with relationships and memory. The response felt more like a personal essay than a list of activities.

Perplexity — Intentional Living

Perplexity organized its answer into a hypothetical day, from morning to night.

Its response included walking, cooking, helping someone, learning something new and spending uninterrupted time with others.

It also introduced an interesting idea: deliberately being a beginner and doing something imperfectly without needing to be immediately useful.

My observation: Perplexity combined practical activities with a theme of being present. Its structured format made the answer easy to follow while still incorporating reflective ideas.

What Surprised Me

Despite differences in tone and structure, all four responses returned to remarkably ordinary human experiences.

Nobody chose to become famous, build an empire or accomplish something extraordinary in 24 hours.

Instead, the responses focused on food, nature, weather, conversations, relationships and everyday moments.

I found the similarity interesting.

However, it’s important to distinguish the language generated by these systems from actual lived experience. These answers don’t establish that an AI system has feelings, desires, consciousness or an understanding of human life comparable to a person’s.

They show how the systems generated hypothetical descriptions in response to an imaginative prompt.

A question like this might seem unrelated to technical delivery, but I think it illustrates several considerations for teams building AI-enabled products.

1. Identical prompts don’t guarantee identical experiences

The four assistants received the same question, yet the structure, language and emphasis varied. In enterprise applications, these differences can influence how users interpret information and interact with an AI-powered feature.

For example, an AI assistant used for customer communication may benefit from a conversational tone, while an internal reporting tool may require concise, consistent and structured output.

The appropriate style depends on the product’s purpose.

2. Evaluation needs to consider the user experience

Technical accuracy is essential in many AI applications, but it isn’t the only consideration.

Teams also need to think about clarity, consistency, tone, usefulness and how users understand the system’s capabilities.

A response can be well-written without being suitable for a particular workflow.

This is why AI evaluation criteria should be defined around actual user needs rather than general impressions of which model sounds better.

3. Human-like communication creates design responsibilities

Several responses described imagined emotions, relationships and personal experiences.

Such language can make AI assistants feel conversational and accessible.

However, product teams should be careful about creating misleading expectations.

An AI system can produce emotionally expressive language without that being evidence of personal emotional experience.

For customer-facing applications, transparent communication and appropriate boundaries are important parts of responsible product design.

What I Would Do Differently in a Formal Evaluation

This experiment was intentionally simple, but a formal model evaluation would require a more systematic approach.

I would:

  • Test multiple prompts representing different user needs.
  • Run each prompt several times to assess variation.
  • Record model versions, settings and evaluation dates.
  • Define criteria such as clarity, relevance, consistency and appropriateness.
  • Involve representative users rather than relying only on personal interpretation.

That would help separate individual writing-style observations from patterns that could meaningfully inform product decisions.

Final Thoughts

What interested me wasn’t which response sounded the most human or which assistant gave the best answer. It was how different interpretations, language choices and response structures shaped the experience of reading each answer.

Choosing an AI model isn’t just about whether it can answer a question. It’s about whether its responses are appropriate for the people, context and purpose the product is designed to serve.

Sometimes, even a simple experiment can reveal questions worth exploring further.
